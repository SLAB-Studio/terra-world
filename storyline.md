# Terra World story bible — Rivergate

## Story promise

Rivergate is already a busy, beautiful town when the player arrives. Families
live there, buses run, children play, shops open, gardens grow, and wildlife
shares the river valley. The town is also imperfect: homes become too hot,
water becomes dirty, waste accumulates, habitats shrink, and residents want
different things.

The player joins Rivergate as its new town helper. They do not win by making the
largest city. They experiment with the environment, observe consequences, and
discover how to make the town a better place to live.

> Small choices can create big changes.

## Leo, the companion

Leo is a curious, warm companion who lives inside the game world. Leo is not a
teacher, chatbot, or source of simulation truth. Leo notices verified changes,
remembers important city milestones, asks short questions, and helps the player
put discoveries into words.

Leo should notice before explaining:

1. “You put that tree close to Maya’s house.”
2. The world shows the afternoon shadow and the room cooling.
3. “Maya says her room feels cooler. What do you think changed?”
4. Only after the child has observed the effect: “The tree blocked some of the
   sunlight and made shade.”

The 0G Agentic ID belongs to **Rivergate**, never to Leo or the child. Leo is the
child-facing voice of Rivergate’s verified memory. The deterministic simulation
decides what happened; Leo may explain it through 0G Compute or a safe authored
fallback.

## Core play rhythm

> Problem → investigate → experiment → consequence → Leo notices → discovery →
> the town changes → a new problem emerges

- Play before reading.
- Discovery before explanation.
- Consequences instead of one correct answer.
- Curiosity instead of quizzes.
- A living continuous town instead of isolated levels.
- Reversible choices without shame or failure walls.

Some consequences are immediate and others appear after the player advances the
simulation to the afternoon, the next day, or the following week. Progress never
depends on waiting in real time or maintaining a streak.

## Rivergate at the beginning

The opening aerial journey must establish a populated town before the first
instruction appears:

- 20–30 visible homes across several neighbourhoods
- a school, clinic, market, playground, park, gardens, and riverfront
- residents walking and chatting; children playing; cyclists and buses moving
- birds, bees, fish, dogs, street trees, benches, crossings, and utility areas
- three to five clearly actionable story locations within the larger community
- open land and construction areas that allow Rivergate to grow later

The world may be visually busy, but the next interaction must remain simple.
Only story locations highlight, accept the current experiment, or show a help
bubble. Decorative places remain believable, animated, and non-interactive.

Leo’s opening line:

> “Welcome to Rivergate! It’s busy, beautiful… and a little mixed-up. Want to
> explore with me?”

## MVP story arc

### Chapter 1 — Where did the light go?

Sunny House is dim and uncomfortable. The player experiments with the position
of trees and nearby objects, then observes how sunlight and shade move. The
discovery is that objects change where light reaches and how warm a place feels.

### Chapter 2 — Something is growing

Maya wants to grow food. Gardens respond differently to water, sunlight, soil,
and shade. The child compares outcomes instead of receiving planting rules in
advance.

### Chapter 3 — What happened to the river?

Waste near the river causes delayed changes to fish, plants, water quality, and
families. The player traces the connection and experiments with prevention,
clean-up, and better material systems.

### Chapter 4 — The missing bees

Nia notices that bees are disappearing. Flowers, vegetation, clean water, and
safe habitat bring pollinators back. The player discovers that they changed an
environment, not merely placed a decoration.

### Chapter 5 — Rivergate is growing

New homes, roads, services, gardens, and businesses create competing needs.
Malik values transport and access; Nia protects habitat; Maya cares about food
and water; Mr. Sam remembers the older riverfront. There are multiple valid
Rivergates, each with visible trade-offs and a physical history of decisions.

Leo closes the arc with:

> “When you arrived, Rivergate was already full of stories. Look how many new
> ones you helped it grow. I think I’ve learned something from you too.”

## Recurring residents

- **Maya** — gardener; cares about food, water, and neighbourhood care.
- **Malik** — transport and infrastructure advocate; cares about access and
  reliable services.
- **Nia** — nature observer; cares about animals, trees, and habitat.
- **Mr. Sam** — older resident; remembers how Rivergate changed over time.

Residents have authored personalities and deterministic needs in the MVP. They
may disagree, but they never manipulate, shame, rank, or profile the child.
Rivergate has one bounded AI companion: Leo.

## Challenge test

Every challenge must answer:

1. What problem can the child see or hear?
2. What can they move, place, remove, or inspect?
3. Which relationships can they experiment with?
4. What changes immediately and after simulated time passes?
5. What trade-off or new problem might emerge?
6. What can Leo ask before explaining?
7. What can the child discover without being told?
8. What physical history remains in Rivergate afterward?

If the player receives the fact before they can discover it, redesign the
challenge.
